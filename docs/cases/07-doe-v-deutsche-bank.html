Doe v. Deutsche Bank

Class-action complaint filed on November 24, 2022

Document type Class-action complaint
Court U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York
Case number 1:22-cv-10018
Source CourtListener
Words (cleaned text) 23,105

This class-action complaint, filed in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York by an anonymous plaintiff, Jane Doe (on her own behalf and on behalf of other women in a similar situation), alleges that Deutsche Bank participated in, facilitated, and financially benefited from Jeffrey Epstein’s international sex trafficking network between 2013 and 2018. The complaint alleges that the financial institution deliberately ignored multiple red flags, bypassed its own monitoring and reputational risk policies, and authorized transfers and structured cash withdrawals that Epstein allegedly used to finance his operation, pay recruitment fees, and buy the silence of the victims. Brought under the Trafficking Victims Protection Act (TVPA), the federal RICO statute, and New York’s Adult Survivors Act, the complaint argues that the bank knowingly prioritized profits over compliance with the law, providing the banking infrastructure that allowed Epstein’s alleged scheme of abuse and exploitation to keep operating unnoticed by the authorities.

Word frequency

Figure 1 shows the most frequent words in the text. The relative frequency shows that no term stands out disproportionately. The most frequent word is “Epstein”, which shows that he is highly mentioned and therefore central to the document; he also appears in forms such as “Epstein’s”. Terms referring to the offenses in which Deutsche Bank was allegedly complicit also stand out, such as “sex”, “trafficking”, “sexual”, and “abuse”. There are also concepts referring to the defendant and to the plaintiff, such as “Deutsche”, “bank”, “Jane”, and “Doe”. Finally, words associated with banking products and activities stand out, such as “accounts” and “financial”, which suggests that these tools were the means through which, according to the complaint, the bank became involved in these offenses.

The word cloud in Figure 2 also shows legal terms such as “U.S.C.”, the abbreviation of United States Code (the compilation of federal laws), and “class”, which refers to a class action. The term “commercial” also stands out, which may refer to different things in this context: on the one hand, to the commercial sex acts within the alleged exploitation network, that is, to these services being offered as a product; on the other, to the commercial relationship between Epstein and the bank, through which the latter allegedly received an economic benefit for providing him with financial services.

In conclusion, the word frequency analysis identifies the main actors of the case: Epstein, the bank, and the plaintiff, the first of whom stands out significantly because of his high frequency throughout the text. The terms also reflect both the offenses in which Deutsche Bank was allegedly complicit and its alleged direct facilitation through the financial and commercial support provided to Epstein. Finally, the text makes repeated use of legal terminology, which is used to set out and characterize the alleged offenses clearly and with a legal basis.

Sentiment analysis

Figure 3 shows that the words “victims” and “abuse” stand out disproportionately, and they strongly characterize the overall tone of the text. In addition, there are terms related to the experiences of those affected, such as “harm”, “abused”, “damages”, and “threats”, which refer directly to their experiences and to the consequences of the alleged criminal scheme. Finally, terms linked to the unlawful conduct attributed to the financial institution stand out, such as “fraud”, “disregard”, “reckless”, and “conspiracy”; this suggests that, according to the complaint, the bank was fully aware of the offenses at issue and remained indifferent to the situation.

By contrast, positive sentiment has few terms, with very low scores compared with those of negative sentiment. The main terms are “wealth”, “engage”, “trust”, “benefits”, “support”, and “benefitted”; however, in this particular context they do not have a directly positive meaning. These terms are used to describe the dynamics of the alleged sex trafficking network: how the victims were recruited, how they were coerced through money and social power, and how, according to the complaint, the bank sustained this structure by granting benefits and directly supporting Epstein in the financial sphere.

Figure 4 shows the dominant emotions in the text. The emotion that stands out from the rest is trust, which is due to the many mentions of institutions and to the use of legal language that evokes this sense of security. The emotion with the next highest percentage is anticipation; this can be attributed to the pursuit of a resolution of the complaint that includes compensation to redress the harm to the victims. Finally, negative emotions such as anger, fear, and sadness also appear to a lesser extent, which are entirely expected reactions given the account of the alleged violence.

The sentiment analysis shows that the overall tone of the text is negative, and that this negative tone has two main parts: on the one hand, the accounts of the victims and the offenses that directly affected them; on the other, the unlawful conduct attributed to the financial institution in handling the finances of this alleged business. The few terms with positive sentiment are words that, in the context of the complaint, do not necessarily have a positive connotation. The strong presence of trust also indicates that references to institutions and legal language are very frequent. Since this is a complaint with an extensive legal basis, the allegations against the institution are grounded in solid legal arguments.

Word relationships

The bigram network was built from the bigrams with a frequency of at least 12, in order to obtain an informative network rather than a noisy one, thus keeping the most relevant information in the text. The analysis uses the largest connected component of the graph, since it concentrates the most relevant information.

Figure 5 shows that the vertices with the highest strength are “sex” and “trafficking”, and the edge connecting them has the highest weight; this indicates that the whole document is organized around this offense, which connects the victims with the bank through Epstein. The word “trafficking” is also related to terms such as “operation” or “enterprise”, which confirms its characterization as an unlawful business and the existence of an underlying structure that, according to the complaint, made the offenses possible. Among the key vertices are also those referring to Epstein, linking him to the alleged sexual abuse.

The connected component of the bigram network identifies the nature of the operation allegedly run by Epstein, making it clear that, according to the complaint, it functioned as a profitable business in which the financial institution was paid for its participation. Notably, the only person appearing in this component is Epstein, and there are no vertices referring to the plaintiff or to the defendant. It can therefore be concluded that Epstein is one of the central axes of the text, the link through which both actors and actions are connected, even though he is not a defendant.

The skipgram network is built with all pairs, regardless of their frequency, which makes it possible to find semantic relationships that are not perceptible at first sight. The largest connected component is also used, since it contains the most important information and allows the computation of statistics that are not defined on a disconnected graph, such as the mean distance between vertices.

Table 1 shows the most frequent skipgrams. Regarding the relative frequency, no combination stands out disproportionately compared with the others. Although two of these skipgrams refer directly to the parties, the plaintiff and the defendant, most of the frequent associations have in common that they refer to the way in which, according to the complaint, the sex trafficking network operated as a business or venture. In this structure, Epstein is the central axis, linked to terms such as “sex” and “trafficking”, which shows that one of the fundamental themes of the document is human trafficking conceived as a business, framed under the TVPA as a sex trafficking “venture”.

Table 2 shows that the mean distance of the network is 4.51; that is, on average, about four and a half steps are needed to go from one word to another. Being a moderate value, it suggests a less centralized narrative, in which the different concepts and actors are connected more gradually throughout the text. This interconnection is supported by the mean degree of 5.86, meaning that each word is directly connected to almost six different terms on average. However, the standard deviation of the degree is very high (12.94) compared with its mean, which reveals great variability: a few words are connected to many others, while most have very few connections. In other words, there are hub words that appear next to many different terms, which points to very marked themes in the text.

Regarding the grouping of words, the clique number is 7, meaning that the largest group of words that are all connected to each other has 7 terms; such a structure usually reflects a frequently repeated central argument or words that always appear together. At the global level, however, the density of the network is close to 0 (it rounds to 0.00), meaning that well under 1% of all possible connections are present; the network is therefore very sparse, as is usual in word networks with a large and varied vocabulary. Consistently with this sparsity, the transitivity is 0.07, so there is only a 7% probability that two words connected to the same term are also connected to each other (forming triangles), which reflects a structure with low cohesion. Finally, the degree assortativity is negative (-0.10), indicating a mild tendency of highly connected words to link with words that have few connections, rather than with other central words.

Table 3 shows the most important words. Notably, the first two vertices, “sex” and “trafficking”, have values far removed from the rest. This suggests that this offense is the basis of the document and that both the actors and the themes revolve around it. Words linking this offense to a profitable activity also appear, such as “venture” and “commercial”, which reinforces the idea that one of the central axes of the text is the framing of the offense as a business model. Finally, terms referring to the bank and to Epstein also stand out, making them key actors in the narrative.

Table 4 presents the words with the highest strength in the five largest clusters obtained with the fast greedy algorithm. Cluster 1 shows a direct relationship with the alleged sex trafficking network and its alleged mastermind, Epstein, and it is one of the central themes given the high strength of its vertices. Cluster 2 groups financial terms linked to banking institutions, associated with the bank’s alleged operations related to the unlawful activities. Cluster 3 refers directly to the institution and to its possessions, highlighting its relevance and economic status in the narrative. Cluster 4 shows the relationship between the plaintiffs and the defendants, showing that this confrontation is constant throughout the complaint. Finally, cluster 9 contains the acronym AML (anti-money laundering); this group reflects the relationship between Epstein and the financial institution, in line with the allegation that the bank ignored risk warnings and thus acted in complicity with the offenses.
